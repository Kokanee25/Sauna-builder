# 04 — Framing & Structure

Build the skeleton: floor, walls, ceiling, and the door rough opening. If you're
finishing inside an existing room, you're framing new partition walls; outdoors,
you're framing a freestanding structure on a foundation.

## 1. Floor

A dry sauna does **not** need a fully waterproofed wet-room floor, but the floor
must tolerate heat, the occasional splash, and mopping.

Options (best → simplest):

1. **Sloped concrete with a floor drain** — ideal, especially outdoors. Slope
   ~¼ in/ft toward the drain.
2. **Sealed concrete slab** — fine for indoor dry saunas; mop to clean.
3. **Tile over backer board** — attractive, durable, easy to clean.
4. **Existing sealed floor** + removable **cedar duckboard** on top for comfort.

Do **not** lay finished hardwood or carpet under a sauna. Whatever the base, top
it with a removable **duckboard** for warmth underfoot and drying airflow.

If framing a raised floor outdoors: use PT joists, exterior sheathing, and a
moisture-tolerant finish floor; provide drainage.

## 2. Walls

Standard light-frame partition walls.

- **Studs:** 2×4 KD-SPF at **16 in (406 mm) on-center.**
- **Plates:** Single bottom plate (use **pressure-treated** where it sits on
  concrete), double or single top plate per your load.
- **Bottom plate on concrete:** put a capillary break (sill gasket / PT) under it.
- **Corners & backing:** add stud backing where benches, heater bracket, guard
  rail, light, and controls will mount — **plan these now** so you have something
  to screw into later.
- **Plumb & square:** check each wall for plumb and the room for square (measure
  diagonals — they should match) before fastening.

### Stud-bay note for the vapor barrier

You'll later wrap the entire interior in foil. Keep stud faces flush and free of
protruding fasteners so the foil lies flat and seals well.

## 3. Ceiling

The ceiling sees the highest temperatures — frame and insulate it well.

- **Joists/rafters:** 2×6 KD-SPF at 16 in OC for a flat ceiling at **7 ft (2.13 m)**.
- The ceiling cavity gets the **most insulation** (R-21+); heat rises, so this is
  where you lose the most. See [`docs/05`](05-insulation-vapor-barrier.md).
- Provide blocking/backing for the light fixture and any heater hangers.

## 4. Door rough opening

- Size the rough opening to your **purchased door unit** (typical sauna door is
  24 in wide; the RO is door width + ~2 in for the jamb + shims, per the door's
  instructions).
- Frame with a header, king studs, jack/trimmer studs, and a sill (or no sill for
  a low threshold).
- **Confirm the door swings OUTWARD** in your layout before framing the opening.
- Position the opening per the floor plan (heater near the door wall).

## 5. Penetrations to rough-in now

Cut/frame for these **before** insulating and paneling:

- **Heater electrical** entry point (with the electrician).
- **Light** location + cable.
- **Control unit** (if external/through-wall) location + low-voltage sensor cable.
- **Ventilation** intake (low, near heater) and exhaust (low/high, opposite wall).
  See [`docs/08`](08-ventilation.md).

## 6. Framing checklist

- [ ] Floor base chosen and moisture-tolerant; duckboard planned
- [ ] PT bottom plate on concrete with capillary break
- [ ] Studs 16 in OC, plumb; room measured square (equal diagonals)
- [ ] Backing added for benches, heater, guard rail, light, controls
- [ ] Ceiling framed at 7 ft with deep cavity for R-21+
- [ ] Door RO sized to the actual door unit, outswing confirmed
- [ ] Vent openings framed (intake + exhaust)
- [ ] Electrical penetrations framed/coordinated with electrician
